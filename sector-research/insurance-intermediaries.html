Executive Summary #

The UK insurance intermediary sector is Amaea's highest-priority Phase 2 expansion target. It is larger than the IFA market by revenue, more fragmented, and currently underserved by compliance-specific SaaS. It shares enough regulatory DNA with the IFA sector — Consumer Duty, SM&CR, vulnerable customer obligations, complaints handling — that Amaea's core compliance-tracking architecture can be adapted rather than rebuilt.

Market headline numbers. Revenue from non-investment insurance distribution reached £26.1 billion in 2024 (up 6.6% year-on-year, per FCA RMAR data). BIBA members alone arrange £150 billion of general insurance premiums annually, employing over 130,000 people across roughly 1,700 regulated firms. Total firm population (directly authorised plus appointed representatives) likely exceeds 5,000 distinct legal entities when the AR network model is included.

Key regulators. FCA is the single regulator. The PRA supervises insurers but not brokers. Lloyd's of London has overlay governance for Lloyd's brokers. There is no separate insurance intermediary regulator equivalent to the PRA.

Top compliance pains. Product Oversight and Governance (POG) attestations and fair value assessments are the dominant near-term pain. The FCA's February 2024 Dear CEO letter and its August 2024 thematic review (TR24/2) found widespread failures across both manufacturers and distributors. GIPP (General Insurance Pricing Practices) renewal-pricing attestations, Consumer Duty outcome monitoring, vulnerable customer protocols, demands and needs documentation, and conflicts of interest management round out the main operational compliance burdens. These are all documentation-heavy, data-intensive processes — exactly the kind of work Amaea automates.

Fit with Amaea. High. The compliance obligations are structurally similar to IFA (Consumer Duty, SM&CR, complaints, vulnerable customers) but with insurance-specific additions (POG/PROD 4, ICOBS, GIPP). Amaea's data model — client journeys, documentation gap detection, outcome tracking, MI generation — maps directly onto what insurance brokers need.

Key risks. ICOBS is meaningfully different from COBS: there is no fact-find culture equivalent, commission economics dominate, and the product governance obligations apply at the manufacturer-distributor interface in a way that has no direct IFA parallel. Amaea will need a sector-specific module rather than a direct port of its IFA product. The broker market is also deeply concentrated at the top (Gallagher, Howden, Marsh, Aon) where enterprise sales cycles are 12-18 months, and consolidation is rapidly shrinking the mid-market. Amaea's sweet spot is the 1,700 or so BIBA-member brokers below the top 20, plus the MGA population. Distribution via broker networks (Cobra, Movo, Brokerbility) could accelerate penetration.

Recommended launch posture. Enter in 2030-2031 as planned. Do not try to force entry earlier. Use 2027-2030 to build relationships (BIBA conference, Insurance Age coverage, domain hire), develop the insurance-specific module in parallel with scaling the IFA product, and identify 3-5 Founders Programme-equivalent early adopters from the mid-market broker segment. Price initially at the IFA Essentials equivalent (£699/mo), building credibility before moving to full-tier pricing.

1. Regulatory Landscape #

1.1 FCA as Regulator #

The Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) is the sole conduct regulator for insurance intermediaries in the UK. Firms distributing insurance products to retail or commercial customers must be either directly authorised by the FCA (with a Financial Services Register entry under regulated activity 'arranging and/or advising on general insurance contracts') or operate as appointed representatives (ARs) under a directly authorised principal.

The FCA's insurance supervisory framework was codified domestically after UK withdrawal from the EU. The FCA's approach post-2023 has become materially more assertive: the 2026 Regulatory Priorities Report replaces over 40 previous portfolio letters with a single consolidated statement of expectations, and the FCA has explicitly stated it will take faster, firmer enforcement action against firms that cannot demonstrate good customer outcomes.

Relevant FCA supervisory functions for brokers:

  • Retail Insurance Supervision — covers most GI brokers and protection intermediaries
  • Wholesale Insurance Supervision — covers Lloyd's brokers and commercial wholesale intermediaries
  • Enforcement — FCA fined two GI broking individuals a combined £1.11 million in 2024; total insurance-related fines in 2024 were £1.1 million (comparatively low, but the FCA has signalled escalation)

FCA's five-year strategy (2025-2030) frames the regulator as "smarter, faster and more assertive." Key themes for insurance intermediaries: consumer understanding and claims handling; data and MI quality; proportionate supervision for firms that evidence good outcomes; faster intervention for those that do not; and regulatory simplification (December 2025 policy statement removed the mandatory 15-hour annual CPD requirement, giving firms flexibility on competency training).

1.2 ICOBS — Insurance: Conduct of Business Sourcebook #

ICOBS is the primary conduct rulebook for insurance distribution. It applies to all firms arranging or advising on non-investment insurance (general insurance, pure protection) sold to retail customers. Key chapters:

ICOBS 2 — General matters and client communication. Best interests rule: a firm must act honestly, fairly and professionally in accordance with the best interests of its customer. Applies regardless of whether the sale is advised or non-advised.

ICOBS 4 — Information requirements. Firms must provide information about themselves, their remuneration, and whether they give advice. Following IDD implementation, firms must specify the nature of their service (advised/non-advised), disclose conflicts of interest, and provide the Insurance Product Information Document (IPID).

ICOBS 5 — Demands and needs (ICOBS 5.2). Before conclusion of any contract of insurance, a firm must specify, on the basis of information obtained from the customer, the demands and needs of that customer. A Statement of Demands and Needs (SDN) must be provided prior to contract conclusion. For non-advised sales the SDN confirms the product is consistent with stated demands; for advised sales it includes a personal recommendation. This is the insurance equivalent of the IFA suitability letter — less detailed, but equally mandatory and equally prone to documentation gaps.

ICOBS 6 — Product information. Includes requirements on renewals, changes to cover, and — critically — the GIPP-linked rules in ICOBS 6B governing renewal pricing for motor and home insurance products.

ICOBS 7 — Cancellation. Governs the 14-day cooling-off period and associated documentation.

ICOBS 8 — Claims handling. Insurers and firms acting in claims handling must not reject claims unreasonably, must handle claims fairly and promptly, and must keep claimants appropriately informed. This chapter is increasingly scrutinised: the FCA's 2026 priorities explicitly flag claims handling oversight as an active supervisory area.

1.3 IDD — Insurance Distribution Directive and UK Onshoring #

The EU's Insurance Distribution Directive (IDD) came into force in the UK in October 2018, replacing the Insurance Mediation Directive. Post-Brexit, the IDD was onshored into domestic law via the Insurance Distribution (Amendment) (EU Exit) Regulations 2019, and the FCA subsequently replaced retained EU law delegated regulations with its own rules and guidance (PS23/18: Smarter Regulatory Framework, changes effective 5 April 2024).

Key IDD-derived requirements that remain in UK law:

  • Mandatory pre-contractual disclosure (IPID for non-life products)
  • Demands and needs assessment
  • Conflicts of interest disclosure and management
  • Product oversight and governance (PROD 4 — see below)
  • Professional and organisational requirements (fitness, training)
  • Remuneration transparency
  • Cross-selling requirements (firms selling packages must make each component available separately)

Post-April 2024, the UK has formally separated its insurance distribution rulebook from EU law. The FCA continues to consult on further simplification; the direction of travel is towards relying more heavily on the Consumer Duty as the overarching framework, with more prescriptive ICOBS rules potentially being retracted or simplified over the 2026-2028 period. Amaea should monitor this — it creates risk if the compliance framework narrows significantly, but more likely it creates opportunity as firms struggle to self-interpret principles-based obligations.

1.4 Product Oversight and Governance (POG) — PROD 4 #

PROD 4 (part of the FCA Product Intervention and Product Governance Sourcebook) governs how insurance products are designed, approved, distributed and reviewed. It applies to both manufacturers (insurers, MGAs creating products) and distributors (brokers placing those products with customers).

Manufacturer obligations (PROD 4.2):

  • Identify a target market for each product
  • Stress-test products against the target market
  • Conduct Fair Value Assessments (FVAs) demonstrating the product provides fair value within the target market
  • Establish, operate and review a Product Approval Process (PAP) with a senior manager attestation
  • Produce and maintain Product Distribution Arrangements (PDAs) communicating target market, distribution strategy, and value information to distributors
  • Review products regularly (at minimum annually) and when material events occur (significant claims trends, market changes)

Distributor obligations (PROD 4.3):

  • Obtain from manufacturers sufficient information about the product, target market, and PDAs
  • Ensure distribution strategies remain consistent with the manufacturer's identified target market
  • Produce MI on their own distribution activities — particularly on remuneration, how their fees/commission interact with fair value, and whether their service delivers value commensurate with their remuneration
  • Communicate relevant distribution data back to manufacturers (complaints, customer profile information, etc.)

FCA thematic review TR24/2 (August 2024) — headline findings: The FCA reviewed 28 manufacturers and 39 distributors. The findings were described by the FCA as "disappointing," continuing a pattern of shortcomings first highlighted in the February 2024 Dear CEO letter. Specific failures identified:

  • Distributors frequently could not demonstrate how they had obtained and applied manufacturer PDA information
  • Insufficient MI produced by distributors on how their remuneration interacted with fair value assessments
  • Firms co-manufacturing products unable to demonstrate they had met all PROD 4.2 requirements
  • Governance processes around Product Distribution Arrangements lacked appropriate senior management oversight and challenge
  • Fair value attestations signed by senior managers without adequate underlying evidence

FCA enforcement consequence: The FCA is requiring firms to withdraw products where it has material fair value concerns. This is a significant escalation from guidance to direct intervention.

Amaea opportunity: POG attestation workflow management — tracking annual review cycles, maintaining the evidence trail for FVAs, flagging when PDAs have not been reviewed within required timeframes, and producing the board-reportable MI the FCA expects — is a direct automation target.

1.5 General Insurance Pricing Practices (GIPP) #

GIPP rules came into force on 1 January 2022 following FCA Policy Statements PS21/5 and PS21/11. They apply to personal lines motor and home insurance products only (not commercial, life, or protection).

Core requirements:

  • Price-walking ban: The renewal price offered to an existing customer may not exceed the equivalent new business price (ENBP) — i.e., what a new customer with identical risk characteristics would be quoted through the same distribution channel. The ENBP calculation must include the value of new-customer incentives (discounts, vouchers, free months).
  • Annual senior manager attestation: A Senior Manager must attest annually that the firm has complied, and will on an ongoing basis comply, with the ICOBS 6B renewal pricing requirements.
  • Fair value for add-ons: Add-on products sold alongside motor and home policies must also deliver fair value.
  • Pricing governance: Firms must have adequate systems and controls to monitor ongoing compliance.

FCA multi-firm GIPP review (2022-2023): The FCA conducted a review to assess whether firms had implemented appropriate systems and controls following the January 2022 implementation. Findings highlighted that some firms had inadequate pricing controls, particularly where pricing models were complex or where multiple distribution channels created ENBP calculation challenges.

FCA evaluation (EP25/2, 2025): The FCA published an evaluation of the GIPP remedies in 2025. The evaluation found that the price-walking ban had materially reduced the extent to which loyal customers were paying premiums above equivalent new business prices, but noted ongoing challenges in multi-channel environments and for customers who did not engage at renewal.

Amaea opportunity: Annual attestation workflow management, pricing monitoring alerts (flagging when renewal data needs ENBP validation), and audit-ready evidence trails for the senior manager attestation. The attestation is a recurring, high-stakes obligation with personal liability for the attesting senior manager — exactly the type of workflow Amaea should own.

1.6 Consumer Duty Applied to Insurance Distribution (PRIN 2A) #

Consumer Duty (PRIN 2A) applies to all FCA-regulated firms with a role in the distribution chain for retail products, including insurance intermediaries. It came into force on 31 July 2023 for open products and 31 July 2024 for closed products.

The four outcomes for insurance intermediaries:

  1. Products and Services: Firms must distribute only products that meet the needs of the target market they serve. Where a broker identifies that a product is not delivering good outcomes for its customers, it must take action (including ceasing distribution).
  2. Price and Value: Firms must be able to demonstrate that the total price paid by customers — including their own commission or fees — is reasonable relative to the benefits delivered. This directly links to POG fair value.
  3. Consumer Understanding: Demands and needs statements, IPIDs, renewal communications, and any other customer-facing materials must be clear, not misleading, and appropriate for the customer's level of sophistication. The FCA's September 2023 Dear CEO letter for retail insurance specifically highlighted concerns about sales practices causing poor consumer understanding.
  4. Consumer Support: Firms must provide timely, accessible support. For insurance this includes how customers are handled at claims, at renewal, and when they are in financial difficulty.

FCA Dear CEO — September 2023 (Retail Insurance): This letter was sent to retail insurance intermediaries and set out priorities for 2023-2025:

  • Ensuring sales practices do not cause poor customer outcomes (specific concern: customers buying products that do not match their needs)
  • Monitoring and evidencing consumer outcomes under Consumer Duty
  • Ensuring adequate MI is in place to assess whether customers are receiving fair value
  • Addressing the treatment of consumers in vulnerable circumstances

FCA Dear CEO — February 2024 (Product Governance): Addressed to insurance manufacturers but with direct implications for distributors who must receive and act on manufacturer information. Key FCA concern: firms signing attestations without being able to evidence the underlying analysis.

Ongoing (2026): The FCA's February 2026 Regulatory Priorities Report confirmed Consumer Duty remains a live supervisory priority for insurance intermediaries, with particular focus on consumer understanding (sales process analysis in Q1 2026) and delegated authority / remuneration oversight (Q2 2026 expansion, findings expected Q3 2027).

1.7 SM&CR for Insurance Intermediaries #

The Senior Managers and Certification Regime (SM&CR) has applied to FCA solo-regulated insurance intermediaries (brokers, MGAs, other intermediaries) since 9 December 2019.

Three firm categories (proportionate):

  • Enhanced firms: Largest and most complex intermediaries. Subject to the fullest SM&CR obligations including all prescribed Senior Management Functions (SMFs), enhanced conduct rules for all staff, and enhanced Individual Accountability requirements.
  • Core firms: The majority of mid-market and SME brokers. Must designate SMFs, conduct annual fit and proper assessments, maintain Statements of Responsibilities, and apply Conduct Rules to all staff.
  • Limited Scope firms: Smaller firms with limited regulatory permissions. Exempt from some baseline requirements.

Key ongoing obligations:

  • Annual fit and proper assessments for all SMFs and Certified Persons
  • Maintenance of Statements of Responsibilities (SoRs) for each SMF
  • Training on Conduct Rules for all staff (Individual Conduct Rules; Senior Manager Conduct Rules for SMFs)
  • Regulatory references for leavers and joiners in certified functions
  • Maintenance of Management Responsibilities Maps (Enhanced firms only)

SM&CR creates significant ongoing administrative burden, particularly for multi-site or growing brokers where SMF responsibilities shift as the business changes. The annual fit and proper cycle is a direct Amaea workflow target.

1.8 Claims Handling Expectations and Oversight #

ICOBS 8 and the Consumer Duty create joint obligations on brokers who handle claims on behalf of customers. The FCA's attention to claims has intensified:

  • The FCA conducted a claims handling thematic review (August 2024), emphasising that claims data — handling times, frequency, severity, acceptance/decline rates — should be actively used in POG reviews and fair value assessments
  • Delegated authority arrangements where brokers handle claims on behalf of underwriters are specifically flagged for Q2 2026 oversight review
  • The FCA has stated it will conduct "continued supervisory and enforcement action in home and travel insurance markets"

For pure GI brokers, claims handling is often outsourced or delegated. The compliance question is whether the broker can demonstrate oversight of the outsourced function, including appropriate monitoring MI.

1.9 Vulnerable Customer Obligations #

FCA Finalised Guidance FG21/1 (February 2021) sets out expectations for all firms. Applied to insurance intermediaries, it requires:

  • Identification: Systems and processes to identify customers who may be vulnerable (health, financial resilience, life events, capability)
  • Response: Staff trained to recognise vulnerability signals and adapt communication, product recommendation, and support accordingly
  • Outcome monitoring: MI demonstrating that vulnerable customers receive outcomes equivalent to non-vulnerable customers; flagging where they do not
  • Documentation: Records of how vulnerability was identified, what action was taken, and what outcome was delivered

March 2025 FCA vulnerability review findings: The FCA assessed how firms had implemented FG21/1 since 2021. Key findings: vulnerable customers continue to experience challenges, particularly those with multiple vulnerability drivers. The FCA found examples of good practice (tailored support, adjusted communication) but concluded that existing guidance remains appropriate — no new requirements were introduced at that time. However, the FCA signalled it will continue monitoring closely.

The practical compliance challenge for insurance brokers (particularly non-advised GI brokers) is that they often have minimal customer interaction beyond the point of sale and renewal. Identifying vulnerability in a transactional, online-first environment requires system-level triggers and workflow — something Amaea can provide.

1.10 Recent Enforcement and Regulatory Signals #

FCA enforcement in insurance (2024): Total insurance-related FCA fines in 2024 were £1.11 million, levied against two GI broking individuals. This is low in absolute terms but the FCA has explicitly stated it will use "the full range of regulatory tools" for firms that cannot demonstrate fair value compliance. Product withdrawal orders (ordering insurers and intermediaries to stop selling products) have become an active enforcement tool.

FCA February 2026 Regulatory Priorities Report (Insurance): This is the most significant recent regulatory publication for Amaea's research. Key implications:

  • Replaces 40+ portfolio letters — consolidates expectations in one annually-updated document
  • Q1 2026: FCA will analyse how different sales processes affect consumer outcomes
  • Q2 2026: FCA will expand oversight to include delegated authority models and remuneration arrangements
  • Q4 2026: Data and reporting review may change the nature and volume of data intermediaries must report
  • Firms demonstrating consistently good outcomes will receive lighter-touch supervision; others face faster intervention

Pure Protection Market Study MS24/1 (ongoing): Launched in 2024, interim report published, final report expected Q3 2026. Key interim finding: approximately 12,000 intermediaries active in the pure protection market; 92% of income protection and 83% of critical illness premiums from new sales are generated through intermediaries. The study examines whether commission structures and distribution practices deliver good consumer outcomes. Final recommendations could impose new obligations on protection intermediaries in 2026-2027.

2. Market Sizing — TAM / SAM / SOM #

2.1 Firm Population #

Total market (FCA-authorised insurance intermediaries):

Based on FCA data, approximately 12,000 firms submit at least one element of the RMAR annually, though this number includes firms conducting insurance distribution as a secondary activity alongside mortgage broking, retail investment advice, or consumer credit. The directly addressable population of dedicated insurance intermediary firms is smaller.

From the FCA's May 2025 FOI release:

  • Newly directly authorised GI intermediaries approved per year: 75-175 (2020-2024), suggesting a meaningful stock of directly authorised firms
  • AR approvals per year: 1,644-2,784 (2020-2024), suggesting a large AR population operating under principal firms

Estimates of total firm types:

Segment Estimated UK Firm Count Notes
BIBA member firms ~1,700 Directly authorised GI brokers; BIBA's own figure
Non-BIBA directly authorised GI intermediaries ~2,000-3,000 Estimate based on register activity [needs verification]
Lloyd's brokers (LIIBA members) ~200+ LIIBA represents "vast majority" of Lloyd's brokers
MGAs (MGAA members) 233 MGAA 2024 membership data; ~350 total MGAs in market
Pure protection intermediaries ~12,000 active in market Per FCA MS24/1 interim report; many are IFAs with protection capability
Appointed Representatives (GI) ~15,000-20,000 Based on AR approval volumes over time [estimate]

Realistic directly authorised firm count: Approximately 5,000-6,000 directly authorised insurance intermediary firms (excluding the AR tail and the broader protection intermediary universe which heavily overlaps with IFAs).

2.2 Revenue and Market Size #

  • Non-investment insurance distribution revenue (2024): £26.1 billion (FCA RMAR data, up 6.6% vs 2023)
  • Commission as share of revenue: 82.6% of the £26.1 billion (approximately £21.6 billion in commission)
  • BIBA member premium volume: £150 billion of general insurance premiums arranged annually
  • MGA GWP (MGAA members): £13.2 billion in GWP; broader UK MGA market ~£47 billion GWP [Genasys estimate]
  • UK SME GI market GWP: £15.52 billion in 2023; broker share ~43.7% of SME premiums, 83.5% of all commercial lines
  • LIIBA member premium placement: £45 billion in London Market + £8 billion globally from London operations
  • Regulatory compliance costs to brokers: Estimated at 8.1% of insurance mediation fees and commissions (BIBA 2023 research), equivalent to ~£2.1 billion annually — the "cost of compliance" that Amaea directly reduces

2.3 TAM / SAM / SOM for Amaea #

TAM (Total Addressable Market): All FCA-regulated insurance intermediary firms capable of paying for compliance SaaS. Approximate population: 5,000 directly authorised firms (excluding the very smallest sole-trader ARs). At Amaea's blended IFA ARPA of ~£2,094/month, TAM = ~£125 million ARR. In practice, insurance ARPA will likely be lower for entry-level at launch (pricing discussed in Section 9).

SAM (Serviceable Addressable Market): Mid-market GI brokers (1-100 staff) and protection intermediaries who do not have in-house compliance teams and are not already locked into enterprise compliance platforms. Estimated population: 3,000-4,000 firms. At a blended entry ARPA of £900/month (reflecting a modest discount to IFA pricing until Amaea builds insurance-specific features): SAM = ~£32-43 million ARR.

SOM (Serviceable Obtainable Market — by 2033): Amaea target: 3-5% market penetration of SAM within 2 years of launch in insurance. At 150-200 insurance broker clients at a blended ARPA of £1,200/month: SOM = £2.2-2.9 million ARR from insurance sector. This is additive to the IFA base and positions Amaea for Series A credibility.

Note: All SOM figures are illustrative planning estimates, not forecasts. They should be stress-tested against actual sales cycle data from the IFA Founders Programme before committing to insurance sales resource.

3. Customer Segmentation #

3.1 Large Commercial Brokers — Not Amaea Targets (Phase 2) #

The top 10 UK brokers by revenue are Gallagher, Howden, Marsh, Aon, Lockton, BMS, Ardonagh, Specialty Group, PIB Group, and similar. Gallagher became the first UK broker to exceed £1 billion in UK non-life broking revenue. These firms have:

  • In-house compliance teams of 10-50+ people
  • Existing enterprise-level technology stacks (often built or customised internally)
  • Long procurement cycles (12-18 months minimum)
  • Procurement driven by group-level IT committees, not compliance officers
  • Pricing expectations in the £50,000-£500,000+ annual contract range

Amaea should not target these firms in Phase 2. The only exception would be if one of these firms has an SME or regional division that operates semi-independently with devolved compliance responsibility — in which case the division could be a Founders Programme candidate.

3.2 Mid-Market Brokers (£10-100m GWP, 50-300 staff) — Primary Target #

This is Amaea's core Phase 2 sweet spot. Characteristics:

  • Compliance function exists but is typically a team of 2-5 people (compliance manager plus juniors)
  • Compliance manager often wears multiple hats (compliance + T&C + risk)
  • Heavily reliant on UKGI Group, RWA, or other external consultants for specialist input
  • POG, Consumer Duty attestations, and annual GIPP renewals create significant manual burden
  • BMS (Acturis/Open GI/SSP) provides policy admin but minimal compliance workflow support
  • Budget authority often with CFO or CCO; compliance manager champions the tool
  • Deal sizes: £1,000-£3,000/month realistic; align to Amaea Professional/Scale tiers

Firms in this band include (see Section 6 for more detail): Jensten, Clear Group, Bollington, Seventeen Group, Marsh Commercial (SME division), Howden SME, Sabre Insurance (distribution arm), Partners&, JMG Group, Clegg Gifford.

3.3 SME Brokers (1-20 staff) — Secondary Target via Networks #

Small regional and specialist brokers (sole traders to ~20 staff) represent the largest number of firms but the smallest individual deal size. Most operate as Appointed Representatives of Cobra, Movo, Brokerbility, or similar networks, meaning:

  • Compliance responsibility is partially delegated to the principal (network)
  • But the AR firm still carries obligations for its own conduct, customer interactions, and documentation
  • Often run by owner-brokers with no dedicated compliance resource
  • Price sensitivity is high; £300-700/month is realistic
  • Network-level distribution is the logical channel: sell to the network, get 50-200 ARs as customers

Segment includes: commercial lines SME brokers in regional towns, personal lines specialists, motor trade specialists, farm and rural specialists, professional indemnity niche brokers.

3.4 Specialist Segments #

Motor and Household (personal lines GI): Highest regulatory burden from GIPP (price-walking ban applies here and not in commercial). Renewal pricing attestations, ENBP calculations, and the consumer understanding obligations at renewal are significant. However, the largest players (Direct Line, Admiral, LV=, etc.) are insurers not brokers, and the broker intermediary population in pure personal lines is relatively small. GIPP-specific compliance module is a differentiator here.

Commercial Lines (SME and mid-market): Largest broker revenue segment. GIPP does not apply. Primary obligations: POG, ICOBS demands and needs, Consumer Duty, SM&CR. Highly fragmented; many regional firms. Good Amaea target.

Marine and Aviation: Highly specialist. Most place through Lloyd's. Primarily LIIBA territory. Sophisticated in-house compliance. Not a near-term Amaea target.

Professional Indemnity: Specialist but often embedded within broader commercial brokers. PI is a high-scrutiny product class with significant consumer protection obligations. Some standalone PI specialists exist (e.g. Lockton, DUAL, Tokio Marine HCC in MGA form).

Protection / Life: Heavily overlaps with IFA sector. Pure protection intermediaries (no investment advice) are subject to ICOBS not COBS, but Consumer Duty, vulnerable customer obligations, and SM&CR apply identically. The FCA's Pure Protection Market Study (MS24/1) is driving regulatory attention to this segment. Cross-sell from IFA is relevant here: many IFA firms have protection-only advisers or run separate protection businesses.

MGAs (Managing General Agents): MGAs are not brokers — they are underwriting agents acting on behalf of insurers. But they are FCA-regulated (for distribution activities), subject to POG as manufacturers/co-manufacturers, and face Lloyd's Blueprint Two compliance requirements if they are Lloyd's MGAs. 233 MGAA members underwrite £13.2 billion GWP. MGAs need POG manufacturer-side tooling and Lloyd's operational requirements. A natural Phase 3 expansion target for Amaea, distinct from broker-side tooling.

3.5 Network Structures #

UK broker networks serve as principal firms for large numbers of AR brokers, taking on regulatory responsibility for their conduct in exchange for fees and/or access to scheme markets. Key networks:

Network Owner Notes
Cobra Network PIB Group Free to join; PIB-backed
Bravo Networks Ardonagh Group Part of Ardonagh ecosystem
Brokerbility Clear Group Recently acquired by Clear
Movo Partnership Independent (member-owned structure) Focused on compliance centralisation
Momentum Broker Solutions Independent AR network with compliance support emphasis

Networks are a critical GTM channel for Amaea. If Amaea becomes the compliance platform of choice for a network principal, it gets the AR base as a near-automatic customer pool. Pricing via networks likely involves a platform-level deal with per-AR unit economics. This is worth modelling before 2030 entry.

5. Compliance Pain Points #

This section maps the specific operational compliance burdens facing insurance broker compliance teams, and identifies where Amaea's AI-assisted workflow can add concrete, demonstrable value.

5.1 POG Attestations and Fair Value Assessments #

The pain: Every product a broker distributes requires a Fair Value Assessment (FVA) demonstrating that the product — including the broker's own commission or fee — provides fair value to customers within the target market. For a mid-market broker distributing 50-200 products across commercial, personal lines, and protection, this creates 50-200 annual review obligations, each requiring:

  • Evidence of the manufacturer's FVA receipt and review
  • Distributor-side FVA covering the broker's own remuneration
  • Board-level sign-off or senior manager attestation
  • MI demonstrating ongoing monitoring (complaints data, claims data, customer outcome data)
  • Archive of the full evidence trail for FCA audit purposes

Current state: Most mid-market brokers manage this via spreadsheets, shared drives, and an annual crunch period. External consultants (UKGI, Branko, ICSR) often run the review process. There is no systematic tracking of which products have been reviewed, when, by whom, and with what evidence.

FCA finding (TR24/2, 2024): Insufficient MI; governance processes lacking senior management challenge; attestations signed without adequate underlying evidence.

Amaea automation: Product registry with review cycle tracking; automated alerts when annual review is due or overdue; evidence upload and tagging against specific PROD 4 requirements; MI dashboard showing coverage rates and outstanding items; senior manager attestation workflow with digital sign-off and audit trail.

5.2 GIPP Renewal Pricing Attestation #

The pain: For personal lines motor and home products, the annual senior manager attestation under ICOBS 6B requires documented evidence that renewal pricing has complied with the ENBP rule throughout the year. This is a personal liability for the attesting Senior Manager.

Current state: Large personal lines brokers have built or bought pricing tools. Smaller firms rely on their BMS renewal feed and informal checks. The attestation itself is often a Word document signed and filed with no systematic evidence management.

Amaea automation: Annual attestation workflow with structured evidence capture; renewal pricing monitoring integration (flagging anomalies); attestation history with signatory records; personal dashboard for the attesting Senior Manager showing their outstanding obligations and evidence status.

5.3 Demands and Needs Documentation #

The pain: Every insurance sale — advised or non-advised — requires a Statement of Demands and Needs (SDN) provided before contract conclusion (ICOBS 5.2). For high-volume non-advised transactional brokers (commercial lines, personal lines), the SDN is often a template checkbox form. For advised protection brokers it resembles a suitability letter. The FCA expects the SDN to be genuinely tailored: "modulated according to the complexity of the contract of insurance proposed."

Current state: SDN templates embedded in BMS systems are frequently generic and not genuinely modulated. Advisers know they exist; compliance teams rarely have visibility of whether they are completed correctly, held on record, or accessible for audit.

Amaea automation: SDN quality monitoring — flagging cases where SDN templates appear to be inadequately completed or where there is a mismatch between declared customer needs and product placed; file sampling workflow for compliance managers; retention tracking to ensure SDNs are held for the required period (typically six years).

5.4 Vulnerable Customer Identification and Outcomes Tracking #

The pain: FG21/1 requires firms to identify vulnerable customers, take appropriate action, and demonstrate through MI that vulnerable customers receive equivalent outcomes. For GI brokers operating in transactional models with minimal ongoing client contact, this is genuinely hard. There is no obvious trigger to flag vulnerability (unlike an IFA where an annual review creates a touchpoint). Renewal-only contacts mean the broker may have one meaningful customer interaction per year per client.

FCA finding (March 2025): Firms in multi-touchpoint sectors generally perform better; transactional, online-first models perform worst. Insurance brokers are disproportionately represented in the worst-performing group.

Amaea automation: Vulnerability flag prompts within the customer journey workflow (integrated into renewal or contact workflows); staff training completion tracking (vulnerability-specific competence); outcome sampling — pulling a random sample of 'flagged vulnerable' cases for compliance manager review quarterly; MI dashboard showing vulnerability identification rates, action rates, and outcome data vs. non-vulnerable customer comparators.

5.5 Conflicts of Interest Management #

The pain: FCA Thematic Review TR14/9 (Conflicts of Interest and Intermediary Remuneration, 2014) found that many brokers — particularly those serving SME clients — relied on disclosure alone to manage conflicts, rather than having active control frameworks. This remains an ongoing FCA concern, codified now through Consumer Duty (PRIN 2A). Specific conflicts include: volume-based incentives from insurers, preferential access to scheme markets, co-manufacture arrangements, and bundled add-on products.

Amaea automation: Conflicts register with ongoing review cycle; remuneration disclosure management; alerts when new products or insurer relationships might create undisclosed conflicts; integration with Product Distribution Arrangements to flag where a distributor's remuneration may be inconsistent with a manufacturer's FVA.

5.6 Complaints Handling #

The pain: ICOBS 8, Consumer Duty, and the FCA's 2026 priorities all emphasise complaints management. For insurance brokers, complaints must be resolved within defined timeframes (Dispute Resolution Rules, DISP), with root cause analysis conducted and MI reported. The FOS (Financial Ombudsman Service) levies costs per case referred. The FCA expects complaints data to feed into POG reviews and fair value assessments.

Amaea automation: Complaints log with statutory timeframe tracking; root cause categorisation (enabling pattern detection); automatic escalation alerts for complaints approaching breach of timeframe; output feeding into POG MI module (showing complaints rate by product as evidence for FVA).

5.7 SM&CR Annual Fit and Proper Assessments #

The pain: Annual fit and proper assessments for all SMFs and Certified Persons, including verification of criminal record checks, regulatory history, and ongoing competence. For a 100-staff broker with 30 certified persons and 3-4 SMFs, this is a recurring annual process generating significant documentation.

Amaea automation: Identical to the IFA product — this is direct portability from the core Amaea module.

5.8 Management Information (MI) Production for Board #

The pain: Consumer Duty requires boards to receive regular MI demonstrating that the firm is delivering good customer outcomes. For insurance brokers, this MI should include: complaints data by product; fair value indicators; vulnerable customer outcome data; renewal retention data; claims acceptance rates (where the broker has oversight); training completion rates. In practice, most mid-market brokers produce MI manually, quarterly, from multiple disconnected data sources.

Amaea automation: Automated MI compilation from internal data sources; templated board pack with Consumer Duty outcomes dashboard; trend analysis and flagging of deteriorating indicators before they become FCA concerns.

6. Competitive Landscape #

6.1 Specialist GI Compliance Consultancies #

These firms provide human-delivered compliance advice, audits, and ongoing support to insurance intermediaries. They are the current dominant model for mid-market and SME brokers.

UKGI Group (formerly RWA + UKGI): Merged in 2023 to create one of the largest compliance consultancies in the GI sector. Provides: compliance consultancy and outsourced compliance, training (Searchlight Insurance Training, The Development Zone e-learning), FCAS (Fixed Cost Advisory Service) retainer model, and specialist support for Lloyd's and London market firms. UKGI is the most likely incumbent to lose clients to Amaea. Their delivery model is consultant-led, not software-driven; they have limited systematic workflow automation. They are well-embedded in the mid-market via long-standing relationships.

Branko (Branko Bjelobaba FCII): Individual compliance consultant / small firm known specifically in GI. Delivers compliance training, audits, and advisory. No software product. Competes on expertise and personal relationship. Not a scalable software competitor but a potential channel partner (consultants who recommend Amaea to clients).

ICSR (now Artex): Acquired by Artex Risk Solutions in 2024. Advisory and resourcing services for insurers, MGAs, Lloyd's firms. Well-regarded in the London market and Lloyd's space. Post-acquisition repositioning may create a gap for smaller broker-focused compliance tools. Not a direct SaaS competitor.

Insurance Compliance Services Ltd (ICS): Described as "leading compliance service provider" supporting all firm sizes. Provides outsourced compliance for traditional brokers, wholesale brokers, Lloyd's, MGAs. Human-delivered service with some digital content delivery. No significant SaaS product.

RWA Business (UKGI Insight): Now part of UKGI Group. Regulatory content and updates via the UKGI Insight platform. Content subscription model rather than workflow software.

Gap Amaea fills vs. all the above: All these players deliver compliance via people, not software. They are expensive (retainer models), non-scalable, and create dependency rather than capability. Amaea replaces the admin burden with software, allowing compliance staff to focus on judgement. Amaea can also be positioned as complementary to consultants — "the platform your compliance consultant actually uses" — creating a channel rather than an adversarial relationship.

6.2 Broker Management Systems (BMS) — Compliance Modules #

The three dominant BMS platforms in UK GI broking are Acturis, Open GI, and Applied Systems / SSP. These systems handle policy administration, etrading, document management, and basic workflow. Their compliance functionality:

Acturis:

  • What it does NOT do: POG attestation workflow, Consumer Duty outcome tracking, fair value evidence management, vulnerable customer MI, GIPP attestation workflow, SM&CR fit and proper assessment management
  • Verdict: Acturis owns the policy and etrading layer. Compliance is an afterthought. Amaea complements Acturis; does not replace it.

Open GI:

  • ~500 intermediary clients; ~one third of UK insurance transactions; £60m revenue (FY 2025)
  • Acquired by Ares Management late 2024; eyes growth in AI tooling (announced AI chatbot deal in 2024)
  • Compliance modules: similar to Acturis — basic workflows, document storage
  • Amaea opportunity: Open GI's AI chatbot announcement suggests they may try to build compliance AI in-house. Monitor. But their core is etrading, not compliance workflow automation.

Applied Systems / SSP:

  • Applied Systems: North America-origin, active in UK; BIBA ITIC founding member
  • SSP: Owned by Volaris Group since 2020; focuses on personal lines
  • Compliance functionality: Basic T&C and complaints modules

Go-Insur (Pancentric): Transparent modular subscription model starting from £1,500/month. Positioned as an Acturis alternative for MGAs. Not compliance-focused.

Verdict on BMS platforms: They are the operating system of the broker. Amaea is the compliance layer. The relationship should be framed as an integration partner, not a competitor. Amaea should build API or data import connections to Acturis and Open GI as a near-term priority when entering the insurance sector.

11. Risks and Unknowns #

These are the things Amaea does not yet know that could materially affect the Phase 2 plan. They should be actively researched and monitored.

1. Regulatory simplification may reduce the compliance burden. The FCA's direction of travel is towards greater reliance on Consumer Duty and less on prescriptive ICOBS rules. If the FCA materially reduces ICOBS obligations (e.g., simplifying SDN requirements, removing PROD 4 prescriptions in favour of Consumer Duty self-assessment), some of the specific workflow modules Amaea builds may lose their justification. Probability: Medium (FCA has signalled simplification but pace is slow); Impact: Medium (Consumer Duty itself creates MI requirements that Amaea can serve).

2. BMS platforms may build compliance modules. Acturis or Open GI could decide to build deeper compliance workflow capability — POG attestation tracking, Consumer Duty MI — as an extension of their existing platforms. Open GI's 2024 AI chatbot deal suggests they are thinking about adjacent functionality. If Acturis builds a POG module, it could be bundled into existing BMS contracts at low incremental cost, pricing Amaea out for brokers already on Acturis. Probability: Medium (BMS platforms have tried compliance add-ons before with limited success; it's not their core competency); Impact: High if it happens in target segment.

3. UKGI Group could build a SaaS product. UKGI is the dominant incumbent in broker compliance consultancy. If they decide to productise their services — building a SaaS workflow tool that competes with Amaea — they have the customer relationships and domain expertise to move fast. They have acquired Think Compliance (August 2024) and Insurance Development & Training (July 2024), suggesting an appetite for consolidation. Probability: Low-to-medium (consultancies typically resist productising because it disrupts their billable hours model); Impact: High.

4. The mid-market broker population is shrinking. Consolidation by Howden, Ardonagh, PIB, and others is rapidly absorbing mid-market brokers. The firms Amaea would target in 2030 may by then be absorbed into large groups with in-house compliance teams. This is the single most important structural risk. Probability: High (M&A is accelerating, driven by PE capital); Impact: High. Mitigation: Target acquirers as well as acquirees; PIB Group / Cobra Network as a platform deal could be higher value than 50 individual mid-market brokers.

5. Pure Protection Market Study remedies are unknown. The FCA's MS24/1 final report is due Q3 2026. Remedies could include: commission cap or disclosure requirements (affecting intermediary economics), new suitability or fair value requirements (creating compliance burden Amaea can serve), or structural market interventions. The direction is genuinely uncertain. Probability of material new obligations: Medium; Impact: Could be positive (more compliance burden = more Amaea demand) or negative (if remedies reduce the intermediary population).

6. Amaea lacks insurance-sector credibility in 2030. By the time Amaea enters the insurance market, it will have 3 years of IFA client evidence but zero insurance client evidence. Insurance buyers will ask: "Do you understand ICOBS? Do you understand POG? Have you worked with any GI brokers?" Without a domain hire and at least 1-2 reference accounts before public launch, the answer will be unconvincing. Probability of this being a problem: High if not actively mitigated; Impact: Delays first 6-12 months of insurance sales. Mitigation: Domain expert hire by 2028; targeted Founders Programme in insurance sector 12 months before public launch.

7. The AR network model complicates pricing and contracting. A significant portion of the SME broker population operates as Appointed Representatives. Their principal (network) bears ultimate regulatory responsibility, but the AR still needs practical compliance tools for its own conduct. Who pays for the Amaea subscription — the network or the AR? How does Amaea manage the relationship if the network changes its AR's compliance requirements? This needs to be worked through before network-channel deals are pursued. Probability of complexity: High; Impact: Medium (solvable with right commercial structure, but requires deliberate design).

8. Amaea's IFA pricing may anchor expectations unhelpfully. If Amaea enters the insurance market with IFA brand recognition and IFA pricing, large GI brokers may assume the pricing reflects IFA-scale firms (typically 1-10 advisers) rather than GI brokers (who may have 100 clients and 2 compliance staff). The value articulation needs to be insurance-specific. Probability: Medium; Impact: Medium.
