1. Executive Summary #

The UK accountancy sector is the largest planned Phase 2 expansion target for Amaea, with a target launch year of 2031. It is a materially different regulatory environment from the IFA beachhead: accountants are not FCA-authorised in their core work, they are supervised for AML by a fragmented web of professional bodies (ICAEW, ACCA, AAT, CIOT, ICAS, CAI, IFA, CIPFA) plus HMRC as default supervisor, and some hold designated professional body (DPB) status to conduct limited investment business under FSMA Part 20.

Market size: Approximately 35,000 active accountancy firms in the UK employ over 400,000 professionals. The sector generates roughly £38.7bn in annual fees (IBISWorld, 2024). The primary Amaea target — SME firms with 2–20 staff, no dedicated compliance team — numbers approximately 25,000–28,000 firms.

Top compliance pains: Ongoing CDD failures (1 in 5 ICAEW-supervised firms fell short in 2024/25), inadequate firm-wide risk assessments, out-of-date AML policies, poor documentation of source-of-funds checks, PEP screening gaps, and SAR under-reporting. OPBAS has flagged persistent supervisory weakness across all professional body supervisors, and the NCA continues to assess accountancy as high risk for money laundering.

Fit with Amaea: Strong. The core product motion — track client compliance status, flag documentation gaps, surface what needs doing — maps directly onto AML CDD lifecycle management. The difference is the regulatory frame shifts from FCA Consumer Duty to MLR 2017 + professional body rules. Amaea's AI layer could provide: automated CDD gap detection, PEP/sanctions alert management, SAR drafting assistance, engagement letter compliance checks, QAD review preparation, and PCRT advisory.

Key risks: (1) AML supervisory reform — the FCA is becoming the single AML supervisor for professional services, with a new AML Supervision Bill expected in the King's Speech summer 2026. The supervisor landscape will look different by 2031 than it does today. (2) Pricing tolerance is likely lower than IFA — SME accountants are cost-sensitive and have many subscriptions. (3) The supervisor plurality makes a single-product positioning harder; Amaea must speak to MLR 2017 obligations generically, not to a single body's rulebook. (4) Practice management incumbents (IRIS, Wolters Kluwer, Bright) are already building AML modules into their existing stacks.

Recommended launch posture: Enter as a specialist AML compliance layer that integrates with existing practice management software rather than competing with it. The wedge is AI-powered CDD tracking, ongoing monitoring automation, and audit-trail documentation — the bits that practice management tools do poorly. Pilot with 5–10 mid-market firms (10–50 staff) for discovery before public launch. Target ICAEW and ACCA members first — largest supervised populations with the clearest monitoring pressure.

2. Regulatory Landscape — The Multi-Supervisor Reality #

2.1 The Core Distinction from IFA #

This is the most important framing point for any Amaea product decision on accountants: accountants are not FCA-regulated in their core accountancy work. An IFA firm holds FCA authorisation, is subject to the FCA Handbook, Consumer Duty, COBS, TC, SYSC, DISP, and SM&CR. An accountancy firm holds no FCA authorisation for its core accountancy, tax advisory, bookkeeping, or audit work. Instead:

  • It is a member of one or more professional bodies (its "recognised qualifying body" for the accounting qualification)
  • For AML purposes, it is supervised by either its professional body (if that body is an approved professional body supervisor under MLR 2017 Schedule 1) or by HMRC as default supervisor
  • For tax work, it is bound by PCRT (Professional Conduct in Relation to Taxation), jointly issued by seven bodies
  • For audit work (if registered), it is additionally supervised by its RSB (recognised supervisory body) — ICAEW, ACCA, ICAS or CAI — and subject to FRC oversight
  • For investment business conducted incidentally to accountancy work, it may hold a DPB (Investment Business) licence from ICAEW or ACCA, giving it FCA-exempted regulated activity status under FSMA Part 20

The absence of FCA authorisation means: no Consumer Duty, no SM&CR, no COBS suitability rules, no RMAR, no FCA supervision visits. The compliance architecture is entirely different.

2.2 Professional Body Supervisors #

The following bodies are approved professional body supervisors (PBSs) for AML purposes under MLR 2017 Schedule 1, supervised in turn by OPBAS (the Office for Professional Body Anti-Money Laundering Supervision, sitting within the FCA):

ICAEW — Institute of Chartered Accountants in England and Wales

  • Largest PBS in the UK. Supervises approximately 9,500–10,000 firms for AML under MLR 2017.
  • Members: approximately 151,761 in the UK (FRC Key Facts 2025)
  • Runs the QAD (Quality Assurance Department) for audit quality monitoring and Practice Assurance for non-audit firms
  • Publishes annual AML Supervision Report (most recent: October 2025, covering 2024/25)
  • Runs 1,185 AML monitoring reviews in 2024/25; 80% compliant/generally compliant
  • DPB status: ICAEW is an FCA-designated DPB, issuing DPB (Investment Business) licences to member firms

ACCA — Association of Chartered Certified Accountants

  • Second-largest PBS. Supervises approximately 7,044 sole practitioners and firms in the UK for AML (plus 630 in Ireland). (ACCA AML Annual Report 2024-25)
  • Members: approximately 223,454 globally, with over 100,000 in the UK
  • AML supervision reviews: approximately 370 in 2023/24
  • DPB status: ACCA holds DPB status under FSMA Part 20

AAT — Association of Accounting Technicians

  • Supervises bookkeepers, accounting technicians, and junior practitioners
  • Member count: approximately 130,000 members and students globally [needs verification for UK-only 2024 figure]
  • Less well-known as an AML supervisor but approved PBS under MLR 2017
  • Members tend to work within larger practices or as sole practitioners in bookkeeping and basic accounts preparation

CIOT — Chartered Institute of Taxation

  • Supervises tax advisers (Chartered Tax Advisers — CTAs) for AML where tax advisory is the primary service
  • Members: over 20,000 Chartered Tax Advisers in the UK
  • PCRT co-author alongside six other bodies; CIOT's rules effectively set the ethical and compliance frame for tax advisory work
  • Distinct from an accounting qualification; many CIOT members are also ICAEW/ACCA members

ICAS — Institute of Chartered Accountants of Scotland

  • Supervises Scottish chartered accountants for AML; RSB for audit
  • Members: approximately 22,606 (2024)
  • Historically focused on Scotland but members practice throughout the UK

CAI — Chartered Accountants Ireland

  • Based in Ireland; supervises members in Northern Ireland and Republic of Ireland
  • UK members (Northern Ireland particularly) subject to MLR 2017 obligations
  • Member numbers: [needs verification — estimate ~6,000 UK members]

IFA — Institute of Financial Accountants

  • Smaller body; supervises IFA members for AML purposes
  • Used by non-chartered practitioners and some international accountants practising in the UK
  • Member numbers: [needs verification]

CIPFA — Chartered Institute of Public Finance and Accountancy

  • Focused on public sector finance; approximately 14,000 members
  • AML obligations apply to members in practice; CIPFA is a smaller PBS
  • Less relevant to Amaea's target: public sector clients, not commercial practices with AML-intensive client bases

Key structural point: Multiple bodies can supervise the same firm if it holds multiple qualifications. A firm with ICAEW-qualified partners and AAT-qualified staff may have ICAEW as the primary AML supervisor by election. This causes complexity but does not change the underlying MLR 2017 obligations.

2.3 HMRC as Default AML Supervisor #

Any accountancy service provider that is not a member of an approved PBS must register with HMRC for AML supervision. HMRC acts as the "default supervisor" — it is not a preferred outcome. HMRC's supervision is generally considered weaker than professional body supervision, but HMRC does impose penalties: between October 2024 and April 2025, HMRC issued 335 MLR penalty notices. Firms supervised by HMRC tend to be unaffiliated bookkeepers, small unqualified tax agents, and firms whose principals hold no recognised UK accountancy qualification.

The number of firms under HMRC AML supervision is significant but not precisely published in the sources reviewed. [Needs verification via HMRC annual supervisory report.]

2.4 MLR 2017 — Core Obligations #

The Money Laundering, Terrorist Financing and Transfer of Funds (Information on the Payer) Regulations 2017 (SI 2017/692), as amended, is the foundational legislation. Key provisions for accountancy firms:

Regulation 18 — Firm-wide risk assessment: Every relevant firm must conduct and document a written risk assessment of its exposure to money laundering and terrorist financing, considering: client profile characteristics, geographic locations, products and services offered, transaction types, and delivery channels. This must be regularly reviewed and updated.

Regulation 28 — Customer due diligence: Firms must apply CDD when establishing a business relationship or carrying out occasional transactions above €15,000. CDD comprises: identifying the customer; verifying identity using reliable, independent source documents; identifying beneficial owners and taking reasonable measures to verify; understanding the business relationship purpose and nature; and conducting ongoing monitoring.

Regulation 33 — Enhanced due diligence (EDD): Required in higher-risk situations: PEPs, high-risk third countries, complex or unusually large transactions without obvious economic purpose, and cases where the business relationship is conducted in unusual circumstances. EDD requires establishing source of funds, source of wealth, and senior management approval.

Regulation 35 — Politically Exposed Persons (PEPs): Firms must have risk-based procedures to determine whether a customer or beneficial owner is a PEP, a family member, or a known close associate. For PEPs, EDD always applies including senior management approval to commence the relationship and ongoing monitoring. Note: PEP definitions in the UK were clarified by guidance in 2023 — domestic PEPs are lower risk than foreign PEPs absent specific risk factors.

Regulation 40 — Record-keeping: CDD documents and supporting evidence, account files, and business correspondence must be retained for five years after the business relationship ends (or five years from completion of an occasional transaction). This is a minimum; some professional body rules require longer.

Regulation 26 — Reporting obligations: Firms must appoint a nominated officer (MLRO equivalent) who receives internal suspicious activity disclosures and determines whether to file a Suspicious Activity Report (SAR) with the National Crime Agency (NCA) via the SAR Online system. Failure to report known or suspected money laundering when there are reasonable grounds is a criminal offence under POCA 2002, carrying up to 14 years imprisonment.

Regulation 21 — Policies, controls and procedures: Firms must establish and maintain risk-sensitive policies, controls and procedures including: customer acceptance policies, CDD and ongoing monitoring, SAR reporting, record-keeping, staff training, and AML compliance monitoring. These must be approved by senior management and communicated to all relevant staff.

Regulation 24 — Staff training: All relevant employees must receive regular AML training. This is a common point of failure in ICAEW monitoring visits.

2.5 Sanctions Screening #

Separate from MLR 2017, firms must comply with the UK sanctions regime under the Sanctions and Anti-Money Laundering Act 2018 (SAMLA). The Office of Financial Sanctions Implementation (OFSI) within HM Treasury administers financial sanctions. Firms must not deal with sanctioned individuals or entities, must freeze sanctioned assets, and must report to OFSI. Accountants who manage client funds, corporate structures, or who are involved in transactions involving sanctioned jurisdictions face material exposure. PEP screening and sanctions screening are typically bundled in AML software tools.

2.6 Suspicious Activity Reports and the NCA #

The NCA's UK Financial Intelligence Unit (UKFIU) receives SARs from the regulated sector. In 2023/24, total SARs received were 872,048 — a 1.5% increase from the prior year. The banking sector accounts for 78.55% of submissions; financial services a further 10.73%. The accountancy sector is a small but growing contributor; exact sector-level breakdown for 2023/24 was not separately reported in sources reviewed. [Needs verification via NCA 2023/24 SARs Annual Report, published March 2025.]

SARs from accountants tend to be low relative to the scale of the profession, which OPBAS and HMRC have highlighted as an under-reporting concern. Accountants are in a position to detect financial crime indicators — unexplained wealth, unusual corporate structures, source of funds inconsistencies — but SAR quality and volume from the sector remains a regulatory concern.

2.7 Trust Registration Service #

The Trust Registration Service (TRS), administered by HMRC, requires most express trusts to register by 1 September 2022 (retrospective deadline), with changes to trust details updated within 90 days. Accountants who act as agents for trustees must register with the TRS themselves and may need to assist clients with registration. When onboarding a client that has trustees, accountants must request a TRS extract and report discrepancies to HMRC. Non-compliance carries a £5,000 fixed penalty. TRS obligations add a layer of CDD complexity for accountants advising on trusts, estates, and wealth structuring.

2.8 Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023 (ECCTA) #

The ECCTA 2023 is the most significant piece of economic crime legislation in a generation. For accountancy firms, key impacts include:

Authorised Corporate Service Providers (ACSPs): From 18 March 2025, accountants and other professional service providers registered for AML can register as ACSPs, allowing them to perform identity verification for Companies House filings on behalf of clients. ACSPs have enhanced record-keeping obligations (7 years) and ongoing compliance requirements with the registrar.

Identity verification at Companies House: From autumn 2025, all directors and persons with significant control (PSCs) for new incorporations must verify their identity. This creates demand for accountants to assist clients with verification — and for AML tools that can process this.

Enhanced transparency requirements: From 1 April 2027, Companies House will mandate iXBRL-tagged accounts filed via commercial software; micro-entities must file balance sheets and profit and loss accounts; small companies must file balance sheets, P&L, and directors' reports. This materially increases the regulatory filing burden and creates workflow opportunity for practice management and compliance tooling.

Failure to prevent fraud (large organisations, from 1 September 2025): Accountants acting as agents for large organisations need to be aware of the corporate offence. For accountancy firms themselves, this is a cultural shift toward proactive fraud prevention documentation.

2.9 Professional Conduct in Relation to Taxation (PCRT) #

PCRT is jointly issued by seven professional bodies: AAT, ACCA, ATT, CIOT, ICAEW, ICAS, and STEP. It governs the ethical and professional obligations of all qualified tax advisers. Updated version effective 1 January 2023. Key PCRT obligations relevant to compliance tooling:

  • Integrity and objectivity in advising on tax positions
  • Prohibition on assisting with tax evasion; strong obligation to advise against arrangements that are clearly contrary to parliamentary intent or are highly artificial
  • Maintaining confidentiality while complying with disclosure obligations
  • Professional competence — members must not undertake work outside their competence

PCRT is enforced by each body separately through its disciplinary procedures. Non-compliance can result in exclusion from the relevant professional body. For accountants offering advisory services, PCRT documentation — the paper trail showing appropriate advice was given — is directly analogous to the suitability documentation that IFA firms must maintain. This is a compliance pain point Amaea's document extraction and flag-raising architecture could address.

2.10 AML Supervisory Reform — The FCA Single Supervisor #

This is the most significant structural change in the accountancy AML landscape and must be tracked closely before the 2031 Amaea launch.

HM Treasury concluded its consultation (November–December 2025) on the powers and duties needed for the FCA to become the single professional services AML supervisor. The FCA will absorb the supervisory functions currently spread across 22 PBSs. A new AML Supervision Bill is expected in the King's Speech summer 2026.

Key implications of the reform:

  • All professional services firms (accountants, legal, TCSPs) will move from multi-PBS supervision to a single FCA register
  • The FCA will apply fit-and-proper assessments to firms and their owners
  • Skilled person requirements and enforcement powers (currently FCA powers over authorised firms) will extend to the professional services population
  • Professional bodies will no longer function as primary AML supervisors — they may retain some compliance support and guidance role, but supervisory authority transfers to the FCA

For Amaea: By 2031, the regulatory frame may look more like FCA supervision than the current PBS patchwork. This is directionally positive — FCA supervision tends to be more rigorous, more documented, and more easily productised. But the transition period (estimated 2026–2030) will be complex. Amaea should track the Bill's progress and draft statutory instruments closely. The risk is building a product in 2029–2030 against the old PBS framework, then finding the FCA has replaced it with a different set of requirements.

2.11 Quality Assurance Reviews #

ICAEW QAD (Quality Assurance Department): Conducts monitoring visits on all ICAEW-regulated audit firms and Practice Assurance scheme members. In 2023/24, nearly 500 audit monitoring visits reviewed 761 audits; 71% were good or generally acceptable. From early 2024, visits moved to a risk-focused cycle rather than a fixed rotation. Firms receive advance notice from QAD's scheduling team. QAD reviews assess: engagement quality, ISQM1 (International Standard on Quality Management 1) implementation, independence, ethics, and technical accuracy. QAD preparation is a genuine pain point — firms need to pull together complete file evidence at short notice.

ACCA monitoring visits: ACCA completed approximately 370 AML compliance reviews in 2023/24. These are separate from audit quality reviews. The ACCA's approach emphasises guidance before enforcement, but formal sanctions do apply.

Practice Assurance (ICAEW): Separate from audit-specific QAD visits, ICAEW's Practice Assurance scheme requires all ICAEW member firms in public practice to have a practice assurance review — an on-site review of policies, procedures, and compliance with ICAEW regulations. This is a QAD visit with a broader scope than audit alone.

3. Market Sizing — TAM / SAM / SOM #

3.1 Total UK Accountancy Firms #

Approximately 35,000 active accountancy firms operate in the UK (VAT Scouts / NimbleFins estimate, 2024), employing over 400,000 professionals. Note: this figure includes all firms — from the Big 4 to sole practitioners — and is an estimate based on Companies House registrations and professional body directories. ICAEW's own Directory of Firms contains details of more than 21,000 firms worldwide (including non-UK), of which the UK majority would be approximately 15,000–18,000 ICAEW-affiliated practices. The sector contributed approximately £63bn to the UK economy in 2024 [estimate based on broader professional services data — needs verification]. IBISWorld places the Accounting and Auditing market size at £38.7bn in 2024, with 1.2% growth, and projected revenue to reach £39.8bn by 2025/26.

3.3 Firm Size Breakdown #

The UK accountancy sector stratifies clearly:

Big 4 (PwC, Deloitte, EY, KPMG): Combined UK fee income exceeds £18bn annually. PwC leads at £6.36bn; Deloitte at £5.68bn; EY at £3.7bn; KPMG at £2.99bn (2024 data). These are not Amaea targets — they have extensive in-house compliance teams, bespoke risk management frameworks, and relationships with the major legal tech and GRC vendors.

Top 10 mid-tier (BDO, Grant Thornton, RSM, Mazars, Evelyn Partners, MHA, Forvis Mazars, Moore, Sumer, Azets): Fee incomes ranging from approximately £140m to £1bn. BDO at ~£1.02bn; Grant Thornton at ~£724m; RSM at ~£543m. These firms have dedicated risk and compliance functions but may be open to specialist tooling for specific workflows. Not primary Amaea targets for launch — long sales cycles, procurement processes.

Top 11–100 (Accountancy Age Top 50+50): Fee incomes ranging from approximately £20m to £140m. Consolidation is a major trend: 44% of top 100 firms engaged in M&A in 2024. Private equity investment impacted 17 firms with a collective 19.8% fee income increase. These firms are mid-market — they have compliance awareness and budget, but in-house compliance teams may be small. Possible Phase 2.5 targets.

SME practices (1–10 partners, 1–50 staff, fee income £100k–£5m): The largest cohort by firm count — estimated 25,000–28,000 firms. These firms have one MLRO (often the managing partner or a designated senior accountant), no dedicated compliance team, and AML compliance is a burden they manage alongside billable client work. This is the primary Amaea target.

Sole practitioners and micro-firms: An estimated 15,000–20,000 sole practitioners in the UK. They face the same MLR 2017 obligations as larger firms but have zero compliance infrastructure. Price sensitivity is highest here. May be better served by a freemium tier or per-check model rather than a monthly SaaS subscription.

3.4 Geographic Distribution #

Accountancy firms are distributed across all UK regions, with the highest concentration in London and the South East, followed by the Midlands, North West, and Scotland. Scottish firms tend to be ICAS-supervised; Northern Irish firms may be CAI or ICAEW. For Amaea's Phase 2, England-first (ICAEW and ACCA dominated) is the natural entry point.

3.5 TAM / SAM / SOM Estimates #

TAM: All UK accountancy firms with AML obligations (~35,000 firms) at a blended monthly ARPA of £150–£400 = approximately £63m–£168m ARR. Note this is a wide range because pricing has not been validated for this sector.

SAM: Firms with 2–50 staff where AML compliance is real but not automated — approximately 20,000–25,000 firms. At £150–£300/month ARPA = approximately £36m–£90m ARR.

SOM (5-year realistic): 500–2,000 firms paying £150–£350/month = approximately £1m–£8.4m ARR from accountancy alone. This is conservative and accounts for competitive intensity from practice management incumbents, pricing compression versus the IFA tier, and a 5-year build-from-launch horizon.

Note on ARPA vs IFA: IFA tier ARPA is ~£2,094/month (blended). Accountancy ARPA will likely be substantially lower — perhaps £150–£350/month — reflecting: (a) smaller average firm size, (b) more price-competitive tooling landscape, (c) lower urgency of compliance (ICAEW fines are modest — 41 firms fined an average of £4,822 each in 2024/25), and (d) the compliance regulatory frame is AML-specific rather than the broader FCA framework Amaea addresses for IFAs.

4. Customer Segmentation #

4.1 Big 4 — Not Amaea Targets #

PwC, Deloitte, EY, and KPMG have internal compliance functions with hundreds of staff, bespoke GRC frameworks, relationships with the large legal tech and GRC platforms (Thomson Reuters, Wolters Kluwer enterprise, Diligent), and procurement processes that take 12–18 months. Not viable for a bootstrapped startup targeting launch in 2031.

4.2 Top 100 / Mid-Tier — Not Initial Targets, Track for Later #

BDO, Grant Thornton, RSM, Mazars, Evelyn Partners, and the broader top 100 have dedicated risk and compliance teams. Sales cycles are 6–12 months. Budget approval involves multiple stakeholders. However, by 2031–2033 (Phase 2.5), if Amaea has demonstrated success in the SME tier, these firms become viable. Some are actively consolidating (M&A) and may need compliance tooling that scales across newly acquired practices — a potential wedge.

4.3 Mid-Market (10–50 staff, fee income £1m–£10m) — Possible Phase 2.5 Targets #

These firms have enough complexity to need systematic compliance management — multiple partners, multiple office locations in some cases, mixed services (accounts, tax, audit where registered, insolvency, corporate finance) — but typically lack a dedicated compliance officer. The MLRO is usually a senior partner doing compliance alongside client work. AML compliance is increasingly on their radar, particularly with OPBAS pressure and the upcoming FCA transition. These firms may also hold DPB licences for investment business, adding an FCA-adjacent compliance dimension. Realistic Amaea customer from approximately Year 2 of the accountancy launch.

4.4 SME Practices (1–10 partners, 1–30 staff) — Primary Amaea Target #

This is the sweet spot: enough clients (typically 100–500) to generate recurring AML compliance workload, no dedicated compliance team, and a practice manager or managing partner who genuinely feels the pain of CDD chasing, monitoring visits preparation, and keeping policies up to date. These firms are currently using: a combination of AMLCC or Veriphy for individual CDD checks, email for document collection, word-processed policy documents, and a spreadsheet to track who has been checked and when. This is the same messy, manual-first approach that IFA firms use for Consumer Duty compliance — the problem Amaea was built to solve.

Profile of the ideal Amaea accountancy customer:

  • 3–15 staff, 1–5 partners
  • ICAEW or ACCA supervised
  • Services: accounts preparation, tax, VAT, some advisory work
  • Client base: 150–400 SME business clients, some HNW personal tax clients
  • MLRO: the managing partner, who spends 2–4 hours per week on AML compliance
  • Current tools: AMLCC or Veriphy (per-check), a shared folder for CDD documents, paper-based or Word AML policy documents
  • Pain: ICAEW monitoring visit coming up, partner not sure their files are in order; one long-standing client whose CDD hasn't been updated in three years; new corporate client with complex beneficial ownership structure they don't know how to document

4.5 Specialist Segments #

Tax-only advisers (CIOT members): High demand for PCRT compliance documentation and SAR guidance. May be sole practitioners or small specialist tax consultancies. If Amaea builds PCRT-specific workflows, this segment becomes accessible. AML obligations apply if they provide tax advice to clients.

Audit-registered firms: Subject to ICAEW QAD visits in addition to AML monitoring. File review pressure is higher and more structured. QAD preparation tooling is a potential wedge. These firms must implement ISQM1, creating a quality management paper-trail need that Amaea's compliance tracking architecture could address.

Insolvency practitioners (IPs): Regulated by ICAEW, ACCA, IPA (Insolvency Practitioners Association). Subject to strict case documentation requirements. Compliance monitoring is regular and document-intensive. Potential niche segment if Amaea builds IP-specific workflows — but a separate product consideration from general accountancy AML.

R&D tax credit specialists: A highly scrutinised segment post-HMRC crackdown on R&D abuse. Firms specialising in R&D claims face significant compliance and professional indemnity pressure. Documentation of the advice given, due diligence on qualifying expenditure, and PCRT compliance are all live pain points. Not core AML but potentially adjacent advisory compliance tooling.

DPB-licensed firms: Firms that hold a DPB (Investment Business) licence from ICAEW or ACCA can conduct limited investment business (e.g. arranging life assurance, managing investments as incidental to accountancy work). These firms have a light FCA-adjacent compliance obligation under the DPB handbook, including keeping records of the exempt regulated activities. DPB compliance reviews are conducted by the professional body. This is the most natural bridge between the IFA and accountancy Amaea products — a DPB-licensed firm needs compliance tooling that spans both AML and investment-business documentation.

6. Compliance Pain Points #

This section documents the specific, researched compliance failures and workflow pain points in UK SME accountancy firms. Amaea's product must address these specifically, not generically.

6.1 Customer Due Diligence — The Dominant Pain #

ICAEW's 2024/25 AML Supervision Report identified CDD failures as the single most common area of non-compliance:

  • 11.9% of firms had ineffective client identification procedures
  • 10.2% of firms had ineffective identity verification procedures
  • 11.6% of firms were not updating CDD throughout client relationships (the most common single finding)
  • 12.6% of firms had ineffective risk assessment documentation

The failure pattern: firms do adequate CDD at onboarding but treat it as a one-time exercise. For long-standing clients (5–10+ years), the original CDD is out of date — passport expired, business structure changed, beneficial ownership changed — and no refresh has been triggered. ICAEW specifically flagged that monitoring visit findings most commonly related to ongoing CDD rather than initial onboarding CDD.

Amaea opportunity: A persistent client record that tracks when CDD was last refreshed, when documents expire (passport, utility bill), and automatically flags when refresh is overdue. Directly analogous to Amaea's annual review tracking for IFAs. This is the core product wedge in accountancy.

6.2 Firm-Wide Risk Assessment #

Many SME accountancy firms do not have a documented, up-to-date firm-wide risk assessment. This is required by MLR 2017 Regulation 18. Common failures: no written document at all; document exists but hasn't been reviewed in 2–3 years; document exists but doesn't reflect actual client base risks (e.g., a firm that has grown into high-value property work still has a low-risk assessment).

Amaea opportunity: An AI-assisted firm-wide risk assessment tool that pulls from the actual client data in the system — client risk scores, geographic exposure, service types — to generate a documented, defensible risk assessment. This is a genuine AI use case: synthesising client-level data into a firm-level document.

6.3 AML Policies and Procedures #

Many SME firms use template AML policies from their professional body or purchased from a compliance consultant — often last updated 2–4 years ago. MLR 2017 requires policies to be regularly reviewed and updated when regulations or firm circumstances change. OPBAS has flagged that many PBSs are themselves not effectively checking policy currency during monitoring visits.

Amaea opportunity: Policy management with version control, review reminders, and AI-assisted gap analysis against current regulatory requirements.

6.4 Beneficial Ownership and Complex Corporate Structures #

The UBO (Ultimate Beneficial Owner) identification requirement is a persistent challenge, especially for:

  • UK companies with complex holding structures
  • Trusts (beneficiaries, settlors, protectors — all relevant persons)
  • Non-UK entities (offshore companies, foreign trusts)
  • Corporate clients where the presented directors are not the real principals

Since the ECCTA 2023 and Companies House reforms, beneficial ownership data is increasingly accessible — but accountants still need to verify and document their own assessment, not just rely on Companies House data.

Amaea opportunity: UBO tracking within the client record, with a structured fields capture for corporate structure documentation and a flag when UBO information hasn't been updated after a Companies House filing change.

6.5 Source of Funds and Source of Wealth #

For higher-risk clients — particularly those involved in property transactions, large corporate restructurings, significant investment activity, or with a PEP dimension — accountants are required to conduct source-of-funds (SoF) and source-of-wealth (SoW) enquiries under EDD. This is frequently under-documented:

  • Where documentation exists, it's often a client-signed form with no verification of the asserted source
  • Accountants often rely on client representations without independent corroboration
  • Files reviewed by QAD or AML monitors frequently show SoF documentation that would not withstand scrutiny

Amaea opportunity: Structured SoF/SoW capture forms with a document attachment workflow and an AI flag that checks whether the asserted source is plausible given other known client facts. This requires domain expertise to build correctly — this is where the domain expert hire (Section 11) is critical.

6.6 SAR Drafting and Tipping-Off Risk #

SAR filing from the accountancy sector is chronically low relative to what OPBAS and the NCA expect. The primary reason: fear of getting it wrong. Accountants worry about:

  • Filing a SAR unnecessarily and damaging a client relationship
  • Failing to file when they should and incurring criminal liability
  • Tipping off the client (illegal under POCA 2002) while trying to understand their affairs
  • Knowing when a "suspicion" reaches the threshold requiring a report versus when it is merely a concern

The SAR portal and DAML (Defence Against Money Laundering) requests are procedurally unfamiliar to most SME accountants.

Amaea opportunity: A structured SAR drafting workflow — question-based, output a draft that the MLRO reviews and submits — with guidance on tipping-off boundaries and DAML request logic. This is the highest legal-sensitivity feature; requires careful wording. The AI here assists drafting, not filing. Filing goes through the MLRO's professional judgement.

6.7 PEP Identification #

Most SME accountancy firms conduct PEP screening at onboarding but do not refresh it. PEP status can change — a client's family member may be appointed to public office after onboarding; a foreign client may return to a politically active role. Sanctions screening is analogously static.

Amaea opportunity: Scheduled PEP and sanctions re-screening, integrated with a third-party data provider (SmartSearch, Veriphy, Credas), with alerts surfaced in the Amaea dashboard. Amaea doesn't need to own the screening data — it needs to own the workflow and audit trail.

6.8 Engagement Letters #

Engagement letters (or letters of engagement — LoEs) are a professional body requirement as well as a good-practice AML tool: they document the scope of the engagement, the client's obligations, and the firm's AML obligations (including that the firm must file a SAR if required). Many SME firms use outdated engagement letter templates that don't reflect current MLR 2017 requirements or PCRT obligations.

Separately, Practice Track and Engager are specialist engagement letter tools used in the accountancy market — both focus on proposal and LoE generation rather than compliance tracking.

Amaea opportunity: Engagement letter compliance check — run an uploaded LoE through the AI to verify it includes required AML clauses, and flag if it's based on an outdated template. Lower complexity than full document extraction, but high value for compliance audit-readiness.

6.9 QAD Review Preparation #

ICAEW QAD and ACCA monitoring visits are significant stress events for SME partners. Firms need to pull together: the firm-wide risk assessment, AML policies, a sample of CDD files for reviewed engagements, training records, SAR decision log (even where no SAR was filed), and evidence of ongoing monitoring. Most SME firms do this manually — creating a file folder, printing documents, hoping the files are up to date.

Amaea opportunity: A QAD Prep Pack — a generated report showing: overall CDD currency across client base, policies and last review date, training completion records, SAR decision log, and flagged items that need fixing before the visit. This is directly analogous to Amaea's FCA Visit Preparation Toolkit for IFAs. The name differs, the architecture is the same.

6.10 Staff Training Records #

MLR 2017 Regulation 24 requires regular staff training. Firms must document who has been trained, when, on what, and keep records. Many SME firms rely on professional body CPD records but don't maintain a separate AML training log.

Amaea opportunity: AML training record management — log training completion dates per staff member, alert when refresher training is due. Low complexity, high compliance value.

6.11 UK GDPR and Client Data #

Accountancy firms handle substantial personal data — tax returns, payroll data, financial statements, beneficial ownership records, source of wealth documentation. UK GDPR obligations apply: lawful basis for processing, data subject rights, retention and deletion schedules, data processor agreements with software vendors. ICO registration (for firms processing personal data) is required. Most SME firms are ICO registered but do not have formal data protection policies that are reviewed regularly.

This is lower urgency for Amaea's initial accountancy wedge — the AML compliance angle is more commercially compelling — but GDPR tooling is adjacent and could be bundled in later.

11. Adjacency to UK IFA #

11.1 What's Similar #

File review culture: Both IFA firms and accountancy practices have regulatory oversight bodies that conduct file reviews — FCA supervision visits for IFAs, QAD and AML monitoring visits for accountants. The underlying compliance motion is the same: maintain complete, up-to-date records that demonstrate regulatory compliance, so that when the reviewer arrives, you can produce them.

Professional body oversight: Both sectors are professionally regulated with continuing professional development requirements, ethical codes, and disciplinary procedures. The accountability structure is similar even if the specific bodies differ.

Documentation-first compliance: Both sectors create and maintain compliance documentation as the primary evidence of good practice. Suitability letters for IFAs; CDD files and AML policies for accountants. Amaea's core product architecture — classify documents, extract fields, raise flags from gaps — is directly applicable.

Annual review obligation analog: IFAs have COBS 9.5 annual review obligations. Accountants have the annual requirement to review and update CDD for ongoing client relationships. Both create a recurring compliance workflow that benefits from automation.

SME firm profile: The ideal IFA customer (2–10 adviser firm, no dedicated compliance officer, managing partner doubles as CO) maps almost exactly onto the ideal accountancy customer (3–10 partner firm, no dedicated MLRO, managing partner doubles as MLRO). Same pain, different regulation.

11.2 What's Different #

No FCA authorisation (core work): The single most important difference. FCA Consumer Duty, COBS, TC, SM&CR — none of these apply to core accountancy work. The regulatory urgency is lower: ICAEW fine averages ~£4,800 vs FCA enforcement that can be existential. This affects willingness to pay for compliance tooling.

No Consumer Duty: Consumer Duty is the primary commercial hook for Amaea's IFA product — it's a new, broad obligation that firms are actively worried about. There is no equivalent in accountancy. MLR 2017 has been in force since 2017 and firms have adapted (imperfectly). The regulatory urgency pitch is harder.

Supervisor plurality: One FCA for IFAs; eight professional body supervisors plus HMRC for accountants. A single-message product positioning ("Amaea for ICAEW firms") risks alienating ACCA-supervised firms. The messaging must be MLR-2017-first, body-agnostic, with body-specific configuration available.

AML-first frame, not outcomes-first: Consumer Duty is about client outcomes — it is inherently forward-looking and client-wellbeing oriented. MLR 2017 is about preventing financial crime — it is backward-looking and documentation-oriented. The compliance mindset of an accountancy MLRO is different from an IFA compliance officer. Product language and UX must shift accordingly.

No SM&CR: Senior Managers and Certification Regime does not apply to accountancy practices (outside of DPB-licensed investment business, which may have limited application). No individual regulatory approval requirements, no conduct rules. The personal accountability lever that makes IFAs particularly receptive to compliance tooling is absent.

Cross-sell opportunity — DPB firms: Some accountancy firms hold both FCA permissions (usually a DPB licence for limited investment business) and conduct accountancy work. These firms — like Old Mill (accountancy plus FCA-authorised financial planning) — are natural cross-sell targets where Amaea could serve both the IFA compliance need and the accountancy AML need from a single platform. These firms exist but are a minority of the accountancy market.

Cross-sell opportunity — associated practices: Many IFA firms have an associated accounting practice under a common umbrella group (the same principals own both). Amaea's IFA customer relationship may open a door to the associated accountancy firm. This is a GTM shortcut worth pursuing.

12. Risks and Unknowns #

12.1 Pricing Tolerance Below IFA (High Risk) #

The evidence strongly suggests accountancy firms will not bear IFA-level pricing. ICAEW fines average ~£4,800 — a small fraction of the FCA enforcement risk that motivates IFA firms to pay for compliance tooling. AMLCC at £350–£950/year is the current market benchmark for standalone AML compliance software. Amaea entering at £199–£299/month (£2,400–£3,600/year) is 3–10x AMLCC on price and requires a compelling ROI story that the product is materially better. Action required: validate pricing with 10+ SME accountancy firms before committing to a pricing architecture.

12.2 AML Supervisory Reform Changes the Landscape Mid-Build #

The FCA single supervisor reform, with a Bill expected summer 2026 and implementation likely 2028–2030, will change the regulatory frame that Amaea is building against. If the FCA imposes FCA-style obligations (SM&CR-equivalent, SYSC-style policies, FCA-branded supervision visits), the urgency and willingness-to-pay in accountancy increases materially — possibly to near-IFA levels. If the FCA's supervision is lighter-touch (just taking over the existing PBS framework without adding new obligations), the urgency stays low.

Action required: Track the AML Supervision Bill through Parliament. The detail of the FCA's new powers will determine whether accountancy becomes a high-urgency market. Review this memo when the Bill receives Royal Assent.

12.3 Practice Management Incumbents Close the Gap #

IRIS, Wolters Kluwer (CCH iFirm), and Bright (BrightManager, TaxCalc) are all moving toward integrated AML compliance modules within existing practice management suites. The SmartSearch/Credas merger and its integration with Bright's platform is a direct move into this space. If these incumbents build AI-powered CDD tracking natively, the Amaea wedge shrinks. Amaea needs to be established with customers and embedded before this happens — which argues for the 2031 launch timeline being tight, not safe.

12.4 Regulatory Knowledge Base Build for Accountancy is a Substantial Effort #

Amaea's IFA product is built on an 11,645-row FCA knowledge base covering the FCA Handbook, FCA publications, and Consumer Duty guidance. Building an equivalent for accountancy — covering MLR 2017 (as amended by 2022, 2023 regulations), all eight professional body AML supervision frameworks, PCRT, OPBAS reports, ICAEW QAD guidance, NCA SAR guidance, OFSI sanctions guidance, and ECCTA 2023 implications — is 3–6 months of dedicated corpus work, plus ongoing maintenance. This is a material build investment. Do not underestimate it.

12.5 No Established Network in Accountancy #

Unlike IFA (where Hasna has Robin Powell, CISI CEO, Intelliflo, SP Advanced), Amaea has no existing relationships in the accountancy market. Building the equivalent network from scratch takes 2–3 years of deliberate community investment. The 2031 launch target should assume this network-building begins in earnest by 2028.

12.6 Dual-Supervisor Complexity for DPB Firms #

Firms holding both a DPB licence and accountancy professional body membership are supervised by both ICAEW/ACCA (AML) and the professional body's DPB compliance function (investment business). A single Amaea product that covers both compliance frames is potentially very valuable but substantially more complex to build correctly. Do not try to cover DPB compliance in the initial accountancy product — that is a later extension.

12.7 Pricing Model Risk #

Per-check pricing (Veriphy model) may be more intuitive for accountants than a monthly SaaS subscription — accountants think in terms of billable time and specific client engagements, not ongoing SaaS subscriptions. Amaea's monthly subscription model (as used in IFA) may face resistance. Consider whether a hybrid model (subscription + per-client credits for verification checks) is needed.
